How Chatbots Understand What You Actually Mean

By Tabish Syed • 2 mins read
Scroll

When you type “I need to move my appointment” into a chatbot, you never say the word “reschedule”. A good chatbot still knows what you want. It does this in a few steps.

Step 1: Break the sentence into pieces

The bot first splits your message into small units called tokens, which are words or parts of words. Computers can’t read words, so each token is turned into a list of numbers.

Step 2: Turn meaning into numbers

These lists of numbers are called embeddings. Words with similar meanings end up with similar numbers, so “move”, “change” and “reschedule” sit close together. That closeness is how a bot can match your message to a task even when you use different words than it was trained on.

Step 3: Find the intent and the details

Next the bot works out two things. The intent is the goal, such as changing a booking. The entities are the details, such as a date, a name or an order number. “Move my appointment to Friday” has one intent (reschedule) and one entity (Friday).

Step 4: Remember the conversation

If you say “make it 5 pm” a moment later, the bot has to remember that “it” means the appointment. Chatbots keep the earlier messages as context, and that context is what lets them follow a conversation instead of answering each message alone.

Why bots still get it wrong

Ambiguous messages cause most failures. “Cancel it” could mean an order, a subscription or a booking. Typos, slang and mixed languages make it harder. A well-built bot asks a short follow-up question instead of guessing.

What makes a chatbot better

Good training examples for every intent, including messy real-world phrasing. A clear fallback reply for when the bot is unsure. Regular review of the conversations where it failed. Each of these improves the bot more than switching to a bigger model.
